Refresh / Content Opportunity Scoring

Jackline Mutheu · Decision-support research artifact

Abstract. This project asks which anonymized content pages should be reviewed first for possible improvement. I framed the question as a binary classification task using an observed proxy label: a page is an opportunity when its trend is down and its average position is greater than 10. On one client-grouped holdout split, trained models outperformed a Week-4 CTR-gap baseline on the same target; Random Forest achieved Precision@50 of 0.320 versus 0.040 for the baseline, while Logistic Regression had the higher ROC-AUC (0.600 versus 0.573). These are single-split, observational results for prioritizing human review, not evidence that a content change will improve outcomes. The recommended use is a guarded review queue with human checks, monitoring, and no automated publishing.

1. Introduction / problem statement

Content teams can have more pages to inspect than time to review them. The decision here is not whether a page should be changed automatically, but which pages merit a human look first. A poor recommendation wastes review capacity and may hide more relevant opportunities. Machine learning may help surface patterns across observable search, content, and engagement signals that a single rule misses.

This is the FlyRank problem this lane came from. FlyRank works across 32 pseudonymous clients in this dataset alone — at that scale, no team can manually re-read every page every month. I selected Refresh / Content Opportunity Scoring as my lane in Week 1 specifically because it sits at that bottleneck: not "is this page good," which requires human judgment, but "which pages are worth a human's limited time first," which is a ranking problem observable signals can support. The rest of this paper is the record of testing whether that support actually holds up once evaluated honestly.

2. Data and public-safety scope

The analysis uses the repository’s anonymized content-refresh dataset, content_refresh_anonymized.csv, with 30,000 page-level rows and 32 pseudonymous clients. The dataset release/version identifier is not documented in the public repository, so none is asserted here. Records contain trailing 90-day aggregates and comparative last-30-day versus previous-30-day signals; it has no real per-row calendar-date column, so no date range is claimed beyond those windows.

Candidate signals include search-volume and competition metadata; content characteristics; 90-day impressions, clicks, sessions, engagement, and scroll activity; content age and update recency; and categorical content and tool metadata. The paper intentionally excludes client names, page URLs, queries, and row-level records. Client IDs are only used internally to make the validation split, never displayed.

Scope: The data can describe observed patterns in this anonymized sample. It cannot show that a proposed content action caused a later ranking, traffic, or revenue outcome.

3. Methodology

Target and baseline

The proxy target is is_opportunity = (trend_direction = down) AND (avg_position > 10). It is an observed decision-support proxy, not a confirmed record of refresh success. The baseline is the earlier hand-written CTR-gap rule: pages with at least 300 impressions receive a score from their positive gap to the CTR expected for their position tier, weighted by impressions.

Models and preprocessing

Logistic Regression was used as the readable model. Random Forest (300 trees, maximum depth 8, minimum leaf size 20) was tested as a nonlinear alternative. Missing numeric values were median-imputed; categorical missing values were labeled missing and one-hot encoded. Logistic Regression additionally standardized numeric inputs.

Validation and leakage checks

A 75/25 GroupShuffleSplit by client ID produced 22,885 training rows from 24 clients and 7,115 validation rows from 8 different clients, with zero client overlap. The naive random split illustrates why this matters: it produced ROC-AUC 0.767 with 31 of 32 clients shared across train and validation, compared with 0.573 for the client-grouped Random Forest split.

Direct label sources were removed: trend_direction, trend_pct, avg_position, position_tier, impressions_last_30d, and impressions_prev_30d. The final model retains clicks and sessions in the two 30-day windows because they are not literal components of the impression-based trend formula; however, they may be correlated with excluded inputs, so soft leakage remains a limitation.

The result should not be simplified to “Random Forest wins.” On this one split it has higher Precision@50 (0.320 vs 0.260), but lower ROC-AUC (0.573 vs 0.600). A team that mainly works a short queue may value the former; a team needing a broader ranking may prefer the more interpretable Logistic Regression.

5. Limitations and honest framing

  • The target proxies observed decline and position; it is not a label for successful content refreshes.
  • Results are from one grouped split and may change with another seed, a larger validation sample, or future data.
  • Retained short-window clicks and sessions can correlate with the excluded impression-window label inputs.
  • Data are aggregated over trailing windows, so seasonal cycles and longer-term behavior are not evaluated.
  • New clients without history, and cross-client comparisons of raw scores, require particular caution.
  • Freshness is not a monotonic rule here: observed opportunity rates were 0.270 (0–30 days), 0.406 (31–90), 0.361 (91–180), and 0.207 (181+).

Never automate: publishing changes, removing/deprioritizing pages, cross-client score comparisons, or treating any flagged page as a guaranteed win. A human must confirm the page’s condition and search-result context before action.
